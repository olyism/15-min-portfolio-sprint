## Problem

### A shared vision means little when every team still makes the same decisions independently.

Waypoint is a fictional B2B platform whose three products share customers but not a coherent setup journey. The proposed scenario has repeated account forms, conflicting terminology and three teams competing for the same platform engineers.

The design leadership challenge is not to redraw every screen. It is to establish a direction that teams can act on while preserving their local expertise. A central team that approves every change would trade inconsistency for a bottleneck.

My role in this example is to frame the customer problem, facilitate choices and make ownership explicit. Product and engineering partners retain responsibility for commercial commitments and technical feasibility. None of the workshops or organisational outcomes described here have taken place.

## Solution

### I made the strategy a small set of decisions with owners, costs and review dates.

The proposed starting point is a shared customer journey, not an organisation chart. Each team contributes friction in its part of the journey and the dependencies it cannot resolve alone. The first joint decision is to improve setup before creating more feature-specific navigation.

**Choose one shared bet.** The first 90 days focus on identity and setup continuity. A visual refresh is deferred unless necessary for that journey. This makes the trade-off visible instead of allowing every team to interpret “coherence” as permission to redesign everything.

**Assign decision rights.** The platform team owns identity contracts. Product squads own local tasks within those contracts. A cross-functional group resolves exceptions using a written decision record, with a deadline and a named decision owner. Design contributes evidence and direction, not a universal veto.

**Fund adoption.** The plan reserves squad capacity for integration, migration and support. Shared components do not eliminate that work. A staged migration begins with one journey slice, then expands after the teams understand the real cost.

**Create a learning rhythm.** A fortnightly review examines customer friction, integration progress and exceptions. It is not a screen approval ceremony. The leadership artifact is a legible portfolio of bets, dependencies and reasons for saying no.

## Impact

### Judge the strategy by coherence and decision speed, not workshop attendance.

This fictional proposal has no measured organisational results. A real strategy engagement should establish a baseline and name its review conditions before announcing success.

- **Customer coherence:** repeated setup steps and conflicting concepts encountered in a shared journey.
- **Delivery:** integration lead time and the number of blocked dependencies.
- **Decision health:** unresolved exceptions, reopened decisions and time to an accountable decision.
- **Team autonomy:** whether squads can make routine decisions without escalating to the lead.

If the shared approach slows work without improving the customer journey, the strategy needs to change. A consistent interface is not enough evidence on its own.

## How I used AI

### AI can reduce preparation work; leadership still requires accountable choices.

A suitable assisted workflow would summarize already approved decision records and identify apparent contradictions for the owners to review. It could prepare workshop material from non-sensitive project information.

It should not score employees, infer performance from communication patterns or make staffing decisions. The strategic choices remain explicit human decisions with named owners and reasons.

## Lessons

### An actionable strategy includes the work you will stop.

A roadmap that adds a shared platform initiative on top of every existing commitment is a wish list. Capacity is part of the strategy, not an implementation detail to discover later.

To adapt this example, show the real tension you resolved, the decision you influenced and how the team’s behaviour changed after the strategy work.
