## Problem

### People need to understand their options before they can choose one.

Plainly is a fictional utility billing service. The scenario concerns a person who cannot pay their latest bill on time. The current concept asks them to apply for a “hardship arrangement” before explaining what that means, what information is required or what happens next.

My proposed remit is the end-to-end content journey: entry point, options, questions, confirmation and follow-up. A warmer headline alone would leave the service just as difficult to navigate.

This is an interface-content example, not financial or legal advice. Eligibility, commitments and policy wording would require validation by the fictional provider’s qualified policy team before any launch.

## Solution

### I designed the information in the order someone needs it.

The opening acknowledges the task without labelling the person. “Need more time to pay?” leads to a short explanation of available next steps. The user can explore support without being forced to disclose personal details first.

**Model the content.** Each option has an action, a consequence, required information and a next-step message. These fields are reusable across web, email and support scripts. That reduces the chance that a confirmation promises something the service cannot deliver.

**Separate exploration from commitment.** Looking at a payment plan is not accepting one. The review state needs the amount, dates, conditions and an explicit confirmation. The browser example stops at an illustrative support request and creates no real arrangement.

**Design recovery at the same time.** If the service cannot load available options, it should explain whether anything was submitted, preserve entered information where appropriate and provide a practical route to assistance. “Something went wrong” leaves all three questions unanswered.

**Test comprehension, not preference.** I would ask participants what happens after continuing, whether they have committed to anything and what they would do if the options do not fit. A preference for the friendlier version would not prove understanding.

## Impact

### The target is an informed next step, not a higher click-through rate.

No user testing or business impact has been measured for this concept. The validation plan combines moderated comprehension sessions and a cautious service pilot.

- **Understanding:** can people describe the next step and any commitment in their own words?
- **Completion:** can they reach an appropriate support route without avoidable disclosure?
- **Support:** do repeat contacts about status and next steps decrease?
- **Harm check:** are people selecting an unsuitable option because the copy makes it sound universally beneficial?

A higher completion rate would be a poor outcome if people misunderstand what they agreed to. The content team should review errors and abandonment with support colleagues before changing the language again.

## How I used AI

### Use generation to surface alternatives, then apply human and policy judgment.

AI could propose alternate labels and stress-test the content model against missing information, translation expansion and unusual states. Content designers would check meaning, voice and service accuracy; policy owners would approve any regulated claims.

I would not ask AI to infer an individual’s financial circumstances or promise that a support option is suitable. The useful work is mapping language to a correct service action.

## Lessons

### A considerate tone needs an equally considerate service underneath it.

A sentence cannot make an inflexible policy humane. The content designer’s contribution includes exposing that mismatch and working with service owners to change the journey.
